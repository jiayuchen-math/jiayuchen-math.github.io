Studying Modular Forms & Arithmetic Geometry

Start here · Choose a text · Practice · Check your understanding · Continue · Full reading list

A modular form is constrained both by holomorphicity and by transformation under a group action. Milne builds the analytic and geometric framework through the upper half-plane and quotient surfaces. Stein emphasizes explicit expansions, bases and Hecke computations. These routes can be paired: a calculation becomes more informative when you can explain the transformation law and the behavior at a cusp that make it legitimate.

What to work on

  • Check the upper-half-plane action of SL2(Z) and state a modular transformation law.
  • Relate translation invariance to a q-expansion and distinguish holomorphy from meromorphy at a cusp.
  • Compute low-order coefficients of level-one Eisenstein and cusp forms.
  • Apply a Hecke coefficient formula with an explicit normalization.

A starting text

Modular Functions and Modular Forms (Elliptic Modular Curves) — James S. Milne. Read section 1, Preliminaries, and section 2, Elliptic Modular Curves as Riemann Surfaces, for the upper half-plane and quotients. Section 4, Modular Functions and Modular Forms, begins on printed page 48; section 5, Hecke Operators, begins on page 67. Use section 3 on elliptic functions only as needed.

Choosing what to read

First derive the formula for the imaginary part of a fractional linear transformation and use it to understand the half-plane action. Check the weight and group before comparing two forms. Compute several Fourier coefficients, but distinguish matching a finite list from proving equality. Use a dimension or structure theorem only after stating its level and weight assumptions. When a Hecke matrix appears, identify its basis and explain which theoretical operator the matrix represents.
