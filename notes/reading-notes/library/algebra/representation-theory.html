Studying Representation Theory & Lie Theory

Start here · Choose a text · Practice · Check your understanding · Continue · Full reading list

A representation translates a group operation into linear transformations, allowing algebraic questions to be studied with invariant subspaces and characters. Etingof and collaborators provide an entry through algebras and finite-group representations. Etingof's Lie-group notes bring smooth structure into the picture. Choose the finite-group route while you are learning decomposition and character calculations; add the Lie route when tangent spaces and smooth maps are already familiar.

What to work on

  • Find invariant subspaces and intertwiners of a finite-dimensional representation.
  • Apply Maschke's theorem over a field of suitable characteristic.
  • Compute a finite group's character table and tensor-product multiplicities.
  • Verify the sl(2) relations in an explicit matrix representation.

A starting text

Introduction to Representation Theory — Pavel Etingof, Oleg Golberg, Sebastian Hensel, Tiankai Liu, Alex Schwendner, Dmitry Vaintrob and Elena Yudovina. Read section 2.3 for representations; 4.1-4.5 and 4.8-4.9 for finite groups and characters. For a first Lie-algebra example use sections 2.9 and 2.15, then the problems in 2.16.

Choosing what to read

Write matrices for a small group action and verify every defining relation. Find invariant subspaces directly before using a decomposition theorem. Compute characters of a cyclic group and a small nonabelian group, checking dimensions and orthogonality where the hypotheses permit. Record the ground field and its characteristic: averaging arguments depend on them. When moving to Lie algebras, differentiate a concrete matrix action and compare the resulting bracket with matrix commutators.

Try these before moving on

Try a proof or calculation before opening the hint. These solutions address the checks below, not the exercises in the linked textbooks.

  1. For E=[[0,1],[0,0]], F=[[0,0],[1,0]] and H=diag(1,-1), verify [H,E]=2E, [H,F]=-2F and [E,F]=H. Prove the resulting action on C squared is irreducible.

    A hint

    Use [A,B]=AB-BA. Any nonzero proper subspace of C squared is a line; an H-invariant line must be an eigenspace of H.

    Work through the solution

    Direct multiplication gives HE=EHE=EHE=E, EH=−EEH=-EEH=−E, HF=−FHF=-FHF=−F, FH=FFH=FFH=F, EF=diag⁡(1,0)EF=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)EF=diag(1,0) and FE=diag⁡(0,1)FE=\operatorname{diag}(0,1)FE=diag(0,1). Subtracting proves the three stated commutator identities, with the convention [A,B]=AB−BA[A,B]=AB-BA[A,B]=AB−BA. These are the defining brackets for the standard basis of sl2(C)\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)sl2​(C).

    If a nonzero proper subspace W⊂C2W\subset\mathbb C^2W⊂C2 were invariant under the action, it would be a line invariant under HHH. For a nonzero generator www, Hw=λwHw=\lambda wHw=λw, so WWW would be one of the two eigenspaces Ce1\mathbb Ce_1Ce1​ or Ce2\mathbb Ce_2Ce2​, since HHH has distinct eigenvalues 1,−11,-11,−1. But Fe1=e2Fe_1=e_2Fe1​=e2​ excludes the first line, and Ee2=e1Ee_2=e_1Ee2​=e1​ excludes the second. No such WWW exists, proving irreducibility.

    If this was the sticking point:

    • Linear Algebra — Review invariant subspaces, eigenspaces and matrix multiplication.
    • Abstract Algebra & Galois Theory — Distinguish a representation from its underlying vector space and operators.

Already comfortable with this? Skip finite-group review if you can build the S3 table and explain the characteristic restriction in Maschke's theorem. The sl(2) example can be skipped if weights and raising/lowering operators are already usable in computations.
