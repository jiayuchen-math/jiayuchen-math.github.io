Studying Linear Algebra

Start here · Choose a text · Practice · Check your understanding · Continue · Full reading list

Row reduction answers computational questions, while vector spaces and linear maps explain why the answers fit together. Hefferon keeps elimination and proof in view; Axler places more weight on operators, invariant subspaces and spectral structure. If you can find a null space but cannot explain rank-nullity, the operator route can expose the gap. If changes of basis still feel mysterious, keep concrete matrices beside that route.

What to work on

  • Compute kernels and images and prove rank-nullity.
  • Express the same map in two bases and derive the change-of-basis formula.
  • Decide diagonalizability using eigenspaces or the minimal polynomial.
  • State the real and complex spectral theorems with the correct hypotheses.

A starting text

Linear Algebra Done Right, fourth edition — Sheldon Axler. Read 3B, Null Spaces and Ranges, and the Change of Basis subsection of 3D. Then use 5B and 5D for the minimal polynomial and diagonalizability; finish with 7A-7B for adjoints and the spectral theorem after reviewing inner products in chapter 6 if needed.

Choosing what to read

Express one map in two bases and derive the coordinate conversion instead of memorizing the formula. Work with differentiation on a finite-dimensional polynomial space, then compare a Jordan block with a real symmetric matrix. State the field and inner product before invoking a spectral theorem. After a calculation, write one sentence explaining which feature belongs to the map itself and which feature depends on your coordinates.

    A hint

    A repeated root of the characteristic polynomial does not by itself decide diagonalizability. Count independent eigenvectors. For the symmetric matrix, test (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) and (1,−1)(1,-1)(1,−1).

Already comfortable with this? Skip the map review if you can explain rank-nullity without row-reduction alone and derive change of basis. Skip the spectral selection only if you can distinguish diagonalizable, normal and self-adjoint operators over the appropriate field.
