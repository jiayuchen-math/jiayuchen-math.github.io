Studying Algebraic Topology & Homotopy Theory

Start here · Choose a text · Practice · Check your understanding · Continue · Full reading list

Algebraic topology attaches computable algebraic objects to spaces while preserving the effect of continuous maps. Hatcher develops fundamental groups and homology with geometric constructions and exercises. May gives a more compact graduate bridge toward homotopy theory. Start with Hatcher when loops, attaching maps and cellular calculations are new; use May when you can already supply those examples while reading a compressed argument.

What to work on

  • Construct induced homomorphisms of fundamental groups.
  • Compute fundamental groups using van Kampen's theorem.
  • Relate connected covering spaces to subgroups with the required hypotheses.
  • Compute integral homology using cellular chains or Mayer-Vietoris.

A starting text

Algebraic Topology — Allen Hatcher. Start with chapter 0 on homotopy and cell complexes. Read 1.1 Basic Constructions, 1.2 Van Kampen's Theorem and 1.3 Covering Spaces; then 2.1 Simplicial and Singular Homology and the Cellular Homology and Mayer-Vietoris subsections of 2.2.

Choosing what to read

Choose a space built from a small number of cells and calculate the same invariant in two ways where possible. In van Kampen, specify the open cover and basepoint information before manipulating generators. For cellular homology, write the boundary maps and check their compositions. Record the map induced by a continuous map, rather than stopping at the groups of the two spaces. Compare homotopy equivalence with equality of homology groups so the invariant's limitations remain visible.

Try these before moving on

Try a proof or calculation before opening the hint. These solutions address the checks below, not the exercises in the linked textbooks.

  1. Using the usual CW structure with one cell in each dimension 0, 1 and 2, compute the integral homology of the real projective plane, including the degree-2 cellular boundary map.

    A hint

    The 1-skeleton is a circle. The attaching map of the 2-cell traverses it twice, while the 1-cell has the same initial and terminal vertex.

    Work through the solution

    Use the disk model of RP2\mathbb{RP}^2RP2 with antipodal boundary points identified. The boundary quotient is RP1≅S1\mathbb{RP}^1\cong S^1RP1≅S1, its 111-skeleton. The attaching map from the boundary of the disk to this circle is the double covering; with compatible orientations it has degree 222. Thus the cellular chain complex over Z\mathbb ZZ is

    0⟶C2=Z→×2C1=Z→0C0=Z⟶0.0\longrightarrow C_2=\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\times2}C_1=\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{0}C_0=\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0.0⟶C2​=Z×2​C1​=Z0​C0​=Z⟶0.

    The degree-one boundary is zero because the single 111-cell has both endpoints at the same 000-cell. Therefore H2=ker⁡(×2)=0H_2=\ker(\times2)=0H2​=ker(×2)=0, H1=Z/2ZH_1=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb ZH1​=Z/2Z, and H0=ZH_0=\mathbb ZH0​=Z. All higher groups vanish because there are no higher cells. Reversing a cell orientation can change the displayed 222 to −2-2−2 but changes neither its kernel nor its image. In particular, the 222-cell does not supply a nonzero integral top-dimensional homology class.

    If this was the sticking point:

    • Homological Algebra — Review computing homology as kernel modulo image.
    • Point-Set Topology — Review quotient spaces and the antipodal disk model.

Already comfortable with this? Skip fundamental-group review if you can carry out van Kampen with valid open sets and track induced maps. Skip the homology selection if you can compute the projective-plane example with integer coefficients and explain why the attaching-map degree matters.
