Studying Probability Theory

Start here · Choose a text · Practice · Check your understanding · Continue · Full reading list

Probability has two entry points here. Grinstead and Snell build intuition through distributions, conditioning, expectation and finite chains using calculus. Lawler compresses the foundations into the language of measurable spaces and integration. The latter is a bridge from graduate real analysis, so its brevity should not be mistaken for an easier first course. Choose the elementary route if conditional probability still feels like a formula detached from a sample space.

What to work on

  • Compute conditional probabilities and distinguish independence from disjointness.
  • Use expectations, variances and indicator variables.
  • State the weak law and central limit theorem with appropriate assumptions.
  • Find a finite Markov chain's stationary distribution and distinguish stationarity from convergence.

A starting text

Grinstead and Snell's Introduction to Probability, CHANCE version dated July 4, 2006 — Charles M. Grinstead and J. Laurie Snell; CHANCE version by Peter G. Doyle. Review 1.2 and 2.2 for distributions and densities; 4.1-4.2 for conditioning; 6.1-6.3 for expectation and variance; 8.1 and 9.1-9.2 for laws of large numbers and central limits; then 11.1 and 11.3-11.4 for finite Markov chains.

Choosing what to read

Describe the experiment and the events before doing arithmetic. For a Bernoulli sample mean, calculate its expectation and variance, then use the variance bound to explain convergence in probability. Later, translate those same objects into random variables on a probability space. Keep almost sure convergence separate from convergence in probability, and test a two-state periodic chain before assuming that every stationary distribution describes a limiting distribution.

  1. For independent Bernoulli(p) variables X1,...,Xn with 0<p<1, compute the expectation and variance of their average and prove convergence in probability to p using Chebyshev's inequality.

    A hint

    Compute one variable's first two moments. Linearity of expectation does not require independence; adding variances does here.

  2. Toss a fair coin twice independently. Let A mean the first toss is heads and B mean at least one toss is heads. Compute P(A), P(B), P(A given B) and P(B given A), and decide whether A and B are independent or disjoint.

    A hint

    Use the four ordered outcomes HH, HT, TH and TT, each with probability 1/4. Conditioning changes the denominator.

    Work through the solution

    The sample space Ω={HH,HT,TH,TT}\Omega=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}Ω={HH,HT,TH,TT} has four equally likely outcomes by fairness and independence of the tosses. Here A={HH,HT}A=\{HH,HT\}A={HH,HT}, B={HH,HT,TH}B=\{HH,HT,TH\}B={HH,HT,TH} and A∩B=AA\cap B=AA∩B=A. Hence P(A)=1/2\mathbb P(A)=1/2P(A)=1/2, P(B)=3/4\mathbb P(B)=3/4P(B)=3/4 and

    P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)P(B)=1/23/4=23,P(B∣A)=1.\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B)}{\mathbb P(B)}=\frac{1/2}{3/4}=\frac23,\qquad \mathbb P(B\mid A)=1.P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​=3/41/2​=32​,P(B∣A)=1.

    The conditioning events have positive probability, so both ratios are defined. The events are not independent because P(A∩B)=1/2≠(1/2)(3/4)=3/8\mathbb P(A\cap B)=1/2\ne(1/2)(3/4)=3/8P(A∩B)=1/2=(1/2)(3/4)=3/8, and not disjoint because their intersection contains two outcomes. Independence of the two tosses does not imply independence of all events constructed from them.

    If this was the sticking point:

    • Combinatorics & Graph Theory — Review ordered sample spaces and counting equally likely outcomes.
    • Set Theory — Review intersections, subsets and disjointness of events.

Already comfortable with this? Skip elementary probability if you can derive the first check and handle conditioning with a specified sample space. Skip Markov-chain review if you can justify convergence using irreducibility and aperiodicity and explain the periodic counterexample.

Condition on a specified event

A conditioning formula is only useful after the sample space is fixed. Two fair tosses already separate three ideas that are easy to confuse: independent trials, independent events and disjoint events.

What to read for this step

Use the July 4, 2006 CHANCE version of Grinstead and Snell named in the course guide. Read §§4.1–4.2 for conditioning, then §§6.1–6.3 for expectation and variance. The practice source links odd-numbered answers; the instructor solution set is not a public complete key.

When elementary conditioning is secure, use the existing Lawler comparison, §§1–3 and Exercise 4.1, to revisit probability spaces and independence. Add real analysis and measure theory before expecting conditional expectation to follow from finite ratios alone. The route introduction also cites a July 22, 2006 CHANCE file: check which edition you opened before using locators.

One example to keep in view

For two fair independent tosses, write Ω={HH,HT,TH,TT}\Omega=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}Ω={HH,HT,TH,TT} with mass 1/41/41/4 on each ordered outcome. Let A={HH,HT}A=\{HH,HT\}A={HH,HT} mean “first toss heads” and B={HH,HT,TH}B=\{HH,HT,TH\}B={HH,HT,TH} mean “at least one heads.”

Conditioning on BBB leaves three outcomes, each with conditional mass 1/31/31/3. Thus P(A∣B)=2/3\mathbb P(A\mid B)=2/3P(A∣B)=2/3, whereas P(B∣A)=1\mathbb P(B\mid A)=1P(B∣A)=1. The tosses are independent; AAA and BBB are not. Use the existing diagram and check for the intersection calculation rather than memorizing the answer 2/32/32/3.

When to review, when to continue

If you can justify both conditional probabilities and distinguish dependence from disjointness, go on to the Bernoulli-average check. If the sample space is uncertain, review ordered outcomes first. Passing these finite checks does not replace the integration theory needed for measure-theoretic probability.

  • Measure preparation and skip check
Editorial choice and revision

Grinstead and Snell lets you calculate before abstracting; Lawler is the change of language afterward. Keep the conditioning example as a reference when sigma-algebras enter, but do not assume the finite formula covers conditioning on a null event.
