A weak derivative is a locally integrable function representing differentiation through integration against smooth test functions.

Chinese translation, usage & terminology

Prerequisite concepts: Lebesgue Integral · Derivative of a Real Function · Distribution in Analysis

Reading routes: Integration, Fourier methods and weak solutions

Overview

Weak differentiation extends classical differentiation by preserving its integration-by-parts identity. The derivative need not exist pointwise everywhere, but must be represented by a locally integrable function. This allows corners while still detecting jumps and other singular variation that pointwise differentiation away from exceptional sets can miss.

The definition supplies the derivative notion used in integer-order Sobolev spaces. It also explains why weak solutions of partial differential equations can satisfy useful identities even when their second classical derivatives do not exist.

Prerequisites

Know local integrability and smooth compactly supported test functions. A function belongs to Lloc1(Ω)L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)Lloc1​(Ω) when it is integrable on every compact subset of the open set Ω\OmegaΩ. A test function φ∈Cc∞(Ω)\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)φ∈Cc∞​(Ω) vanishes near the boundary, so integration by parts introduces no boundary term. Distributions provide the wider setting in which every derivative exists.

Weak derivatives are unique almost everywhere. This article reserves the name for locally integrable representatives; a general distributional derivative may instead be a measure or a more singular distribution. Test pairings here use ordinary products without complex conjugation.

Intuition

Moving the derivative onto a smooth probe replaces a small-scale difference quotient by an averaged identity. The identity still examines every probe, so it contains more information than a derivative calculated at almost every point. A missing jump creates a nonzero contribution for probes crossing the jump, even though differentiation on either side gives zero.

Important properties

For C1C^1C1 functions, integration by parts proves agreement with the classical derivative. For uniqueness, the difference of two candidates integrates to zero against every test function. Convolution locally with a smooth approximate identity makes that difference identically zero on smaller open sets; approximation then shows it vanishes almost everywhere.

Multiplication by a smooth function preserves weak differentiability, with

∂j(ψu)=(∂jψ)u+ψ∂ju.\partial_j(\psi u)=(\partial_j\psi)u+\psi\partial_j u.∂j​(ψu)=(∂j​ψ)u+ψ∂j​u.

The proof inserts ψφ\psi\varphiψφ into the defining identity. Weak mixed derivatives commute whenever the relevant locally integrable representatives exist, because the derivatives on smooth probes commute.

There is also a useful closure principle: if um→uu_m\to uum​→u and ∂jum→v\partial_j u_m\to v∂j​um​→v locally in L1L^1L1, passing to the test identities proves ∂ju=v\partial_j u=v∂j​u=v. This is one reason Sobolev spaces are complete and smooth approximation is effective.

Connections and further reading

Sobolev spaces add LpL^pLp bounds to these identities; trace theorems supply boundary data that interior probes cannot encode. Fourier transformation converts weak differentiation into frequency multiplication in suitable global settings. Hunter, §§3.1–3.4 develops the definition and its relation to distributions.
