An ideal is an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements, making it suitable as the kernel of a quotient map.

Chinese translation, usage & terminology

Prerequisite concepts: Commutative Ring · subgroup

Reading routes: Algebra toward geometry and homological methods · From rings to sheaves and schemes

Overview

An ideal records a collection of expressions that may consistently be declared zero. If aaa becomes zero, every multiple rarara must become zero too. If aaa and bbb become zero, so must their sum and difference. These requirements explain both the ideal axioms and their role in constructing quotient rings. Ideals are also modules over their ambient ring, linking ring theory to linear methods.

Prerequisites

Use commutative rings with identity, additive subgroups, and cosets. For the examples, know polynomial evaluation and modular arithmetic. The definition does not require every ideal to have a single generator, or even finitely many generators.

Intuition

Ideal generation is closure under all consequences obtained by addition and multiplication by ambient elements. For example, imposing x=0x=0x=0 in k[x,y]k[x,y]k[x,y] forces every term divisible by xxx to disappear. It leaves polynomials in yyy, rather than forcing all polynomials without constant term to vanish. The ambient ring and the actual generators both matter.

Connections and further reading

Noetherian rings guarantee finite generation of every ideal, while prime ideals provide the points of the spectrum. For the constructions used here, consult Li, basic ring notions and Stacks, Section 10.3.
