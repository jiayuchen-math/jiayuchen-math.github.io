Conditional expectation averages an integrable random variable while preserving exactly the information in a specified sigma-algebra.

Chinese translation, usage & terminology

Prerequisite concepts: expectation · Probability Space · Conditional Probability

Reading routes: Probability toward martingales and stochastic processes

Overview

Conditional expectation turns partial information into a prediction. Unlike ordinary expectation, which is a number, it is generally a random variable: different observed information can produce different predictions. Its defining requirement is preservation of averages over every event that the information can distinguish.

This formulation handles discrete observations, continuous observations, and information that is not conveniently represented by one numerical measurement. In particular, it does not require dividing by the probability of an individual observed value.

Prerequisites

Work on a probability space (Ω,F,P)(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)(Ω,F,P) with an integrable real random variable XXX, meaning E∣X∣<∞E|X|<\inftyE∣X∣<∞. A sub-sigma-algebra G⊆F\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal FG⊆F represents available information. Measurability with respect to G\mathcal GG means an observation can be determined using that information.

Finite partitions provide the first examples. Lebesgue integration and the Radon–Nikodym theorem explain existence in general.

Definition and conventions

A conditional expectation Y=E[X∣G]Y=E[X\mid\mathcal G]Y=E[X∣G] is an integrable, G\mathcal GG-measurable random variable such that

∫AY dP=∫AX dP(A∈G).\int_A Y\,dP=\int_A X\,dP\qquad(A\in\mathcal G).∫A​YdP=∫A​XdP(A∈G).

Intuition

Imagine hiding the precise outcome while revealing only its partition cell. All outcomes in that cell must receive the same prediction. The integral identity chooses the cell's average, so the prediction has no systematic error on any observable event.

For X∈L2X\in L^2X∈L2, this becomes an exact least-squares statement: E[X∣G]E[X\mid\mathcal G]E[X∣G] minimizes E[(X−Z)2]E[(X-Z)^2]E[(X−Z)2] over G\mathcal GG-measurable Z∈L2Z\in L^2Z∈L2. The second-moment assumption belongs to this interpretation, not to the basic definition.

Examples and non-examples

Let XXX be a fair die result, and reveal whether X≤3X\le3X≤3. The conditional expectation is 222 on {1,2,3}\{1,2,3\}{1,2,3} and 555 on {4,5,6}\{4,5,6\}{4,5,6}. On the first cell its integral is 2(1/2)=12(1/2)=12(1/2)=1, agreeing with (1+2+3)/6(1+2+3)/6(1+2+3)/6.

The constant 7/27/27/2 has the correct overall expectation but fails the first-cell test: its integral there is 7/47/47/4, not 111. Matching a global average is insufficient.

If G\mathcal GG is trivial, the answer is the constant E[X]E[X]E[X]. If XXX is already G\mathcal GG-measurable, the answer is XXX itself. Independence of XXX and G\mathcal GG gives the first conclusion even when G\mathcal GG contains substantial information about other variables.

Important properties

Conditional expectation is linear and preserves positivity and total expectation. If H⊆G\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal GH⊆G, the tower property states

E[E[X∣G]∣H]=E[X∣H].E[E[X\mid\mathcal G]\mid\mathcal H]=E[X\mid\mathcal H].E[E[X∣G]∣H]=E[X∣H].

Nestedness matters: unrelated information sets cannot simply be substituted. For bounded G\mathcal GG-measurable ZZZ, one may pull out the known factor: E[ZX∣G]=ZE[X∣G]E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]=Z E[X\mid\mathcal G]E[ZX∣G]=ZE[X∣G].

Proof idea: verify measurability and the defining integral identities, then invoke uniqueness. Existence instead comes from applying Radon–Nikodym to A↦∫AX dPA\mapsto\int_A X\,dPA↦∫A​XdP on G\mathcal GG. In the square-integrable case, orthogonality of the residual to observable variables proves the least-squares claim.
