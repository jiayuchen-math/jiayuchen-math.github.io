An integral domain is a nonzero commutative ring with identity and no nonzero zero divisors.

Chinese translation, usage & terminology

Prerequisite concepts: Commutative Ring

Overview

An integral domain is a ring where a product can vanish only because one factor vanishes. This condition permits multiplicative cancellation and the construction of fractions. It does not permit division by every nonzero element inside the ring, and it does not guarantee unique factorization.

Prerequisites

Know commutative rings with identity. A zero divisor here means a nonzero element aaa for which ab=0ab=0ab=0 with some nonzero bbb. A unit has a multiplicative inverse within the ring. A field is a nonzero commutative ring in which every nonzero element is a unit.

Definition and conventions

An integral domain is a commutative ring RRR with 1≠01\ne01=0 such that

ab=0⟹a=0 or b=0.ab=0\Longrightarrow a=0\text{ or }b=0.ab=0⟹a=0 or b=0.

The zero ring is excluded. We require ring homomorphisms to preserve identity. Commutativity is part of the definition: division rings and certain noncommutative rings without zero divisors are different concepts.

For a nonzero nonunit rrr, irreducibility says every factorization r=abr=abr=ab has a unit factor. Primality says r∣abr\mid abr∣ab implies r∣ar\mid ar∣a or r∣br\mid br∣b. These properties must not be conflated.

Intuition

Products preserve enough information for cancellation. If a≠0a\ne0a=0, the equation ab=acab=acab=ac forces b=cb=cb=c because a(b−c)=0a(b-c)=0a(b−c)=0. The ring can still lack solutions to ax=1ax=1ax=1: cancellation means that multiplication is injective, not necessarily surjective.

Examples and non-examples

The integers are a domain but not a field, because 222 has no integer inverse. For a field FFF, the polynomial ring F[x]F[x]F[x] is a domain: the leading coefficient of a product is the product of the leading coefficients.

The ring Z/6Z\mathbb Z/6\mathbb ZZ/6Z is not a domain, since the nonzero classes of 222 and 333 multiply to zero. The ring Z[−5]\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]Z[−5​] is a domain as a subring of C\mathbb CC, but unique factorization fails. In particular,

6=2⋅3=(1+−5)(1−−5).6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}).6=2⋅3=(1+−5​)(1−−5​).

The element 222 is irreducible: a nontrivial factorization would require an element of norm two, and a2+5b2=2a^2+5b^2=2a2+5b2=2 has no integer solution. Yet 222 divides this product without dividing either displayed factor, so it is not prime.

Important properties

Every domain embeds in its field of fractions. Fractions a/ba/ba/b have b≠0b\ne0b=0, with a/b=c/da/b=c/da/b=c/d exactly when ad=bcad=bcad=bc. The domain condition makes the natural map injective.

Every finite domain is a field. For nonzero aaa, multiplication by aaa is an injective map on a finite set, hence surjective; some element therefore maps to 111. Finiteness cannot be omitted, as Z\mathbb ZZ demonstrates.

Every prime element is irreducible. The converse holds in unique factorization domains, but the example above disproves it for arbitrary domains.

Connections and further reading

A proper ideal is prime precisely when its quotient is an integral domain. Localization creates domains of fractions with selected denominators. See Milne, Sections 1 and 4 for domains and the factorization distinctions.
