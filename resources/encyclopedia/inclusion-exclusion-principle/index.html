容斥原理

Combinatorics · Undergraduate · Mathematics Resources editorial compilation (AI-assisted)

Inclusion–exclusion counts a finite union by alternating sums of intersection sizes.

Chinese translation, usage & terminology

Prerequisite concepts: addition principle · binomial coefficient

Reading routes: Counting, graph theory and additive methods

Overview

Inclusion–exclusion makes counting possible when natural cases overlap. It replaces the demand for a disjoint classification with a different demand: know how many objects lie in each relevant intersection. The resulting cancellation is exact, rather than an approximation to the amount of overlap.

Prerequisites

Use finite sets, unions, intersections, cardinalities, and binomial coefficients. When counting objects that avoid restrictions, first choose a finite universe containing every allowed and forbidden object. Changing that universe changes the answer.

Intuition

Each object carries a list of the restrictions it meets. Inclusion–exclusion assigns positive and negative contributions to subsets of that list. Objects meeting many restrictions are not treated as independent coincidences: their complete membership pattern determines the cancellation. This explains why knowing only single-set and pairwise counts generally does not suffice when three or more sets overlap.

Examples and non-examples

Consider integers from 1 through 60 divisible by 4 or 9. There are 15 multiples of 4 and 6 multiples of 9. Their common multiples are multiples of 36, so there is exactly one. The union has 15+6−1=2015+6-1=2015+6−1=20 elements; 40 avoid both conditions.

As a non-example of the uncorrected sum rule, take A=B={u,v}A=B=\{u,v\}A=B={u,v}. Adding their sizes gives four, although their union has two elements. Applying inclusion–exclusion gives 2+2−2=22+2-2=22+2−2=2. Distinct indices may name identical sets; the formula still works.

Connections and further reading

The method links restricted counting to generating functions and to inversion on the lattice of subsets. The pointwise cancellation proof also works for indicator functions of finitely many events, followed by expectation. See Keller and Trotter, §7.2, Theorem 7.7 for the complement formulation and its inductive proof.
