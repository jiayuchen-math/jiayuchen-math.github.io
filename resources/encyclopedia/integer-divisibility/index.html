Divisibility asks whether an integer is an exact integer multiple of another, and organizes arithmetic without using fractions.

Overview

Divisibility is the basic language of integer arithmetic. Instead of asking how large two numbers are, it asks whether one can be obtained by repeating the other an integer number of times. This relation supports prime factorization, common divisors, congruences and integer equations. The phrase “integer number” is essential: allowing arbitrary rational multiples would make every nonzero integer divide every integer.

Prerequisites

Use the integers, their addition and multiplication, and the distinction between an integer and a fraction. No notion of prime number is required for the definition. It is useful to know that a product of nonzero integers cannot be zero.

Definition and conventions

For integers a,ba,ba,b, write a∣ba\mid ba∣b if there exists an integer kkk with b=akb=akb=ak. Write a∤ba\nmid ba∤b when no such integer exists. The order is important: the expression says that aaa divides bbb, not the reverse. Every integer divides zero, including zero itself. Conversely, 0∣b0\mid b0∣b holds exactly when b=0b=0b=0. The units 111 and −1-1−1 divide every integer.

Divisibility is reflexive and transitive. It is not antisymmetric on all integers: 3∣−33\mid-33∣−3 and −3∣3-3\mid3−3∣3, although the two integers differ. Restricting to positive integers gives a partial order.

Intuition

The multiples of aaa form the set aZa\mathbb ZaZ. Thus a∣ba\mid ba∣b means membership in a regularly spaced collection, rather than an inequality. A large integer may divide a smaller negative integer, and a smaller positive integer may fail to divide a larger one. Numerical ordering and divisibility ordering answer different questions.

Connections and further reading

Congruence records divisibility of a difference. Common-divisor questions lead to the Euclidean algorithm, while prime divisibility leads to unique factorization. Judson, Section 2.2 develops the foundational arithmetic used in these transitions.
