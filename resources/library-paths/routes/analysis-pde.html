Reach functional-analytic and weak-solution methods from a first rigorous analysis course.

Suppose a membrane is fixed along its boundary and pushed by a distributed force. A model asks for a function uuu satisfying −Δu=f-\Delta u=f−Δu=f inside a bounded region and u=0u=0u=0 at its boundary. For smooth data, differentiation makes sense point by point. But what if the force is merely square integrable, or an approximation converges without its second derivatives converging? This is where functional analysis becomes a way of doing mathematics with the equation, rather than background vocabulary.

The central move is to ask which measurements survive a limit. Integrating against smooth test functions and integrating by parts leads to ∫∇u⋅∇φ=∫fφ\int \nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int f\varphi∫∇u⋅∇φ=∫fφ. The unknown now needs only an appropriate weak first derivative. In the space H01H_0^1H01​, the boundary condition is encoded through approximation by compactly supported smooth functions. Hunter's notes, §§4.1–4.5, explain how the Hilbert space formulation yields existence and uniqueness. Regularity then asks whether the resulting solution is smoother.

First solve one boundary value problem

Start with John K. Hunter's Notes on Partial Differential Equations. Read Chapter 2 on Laplace's equation for an initial supply of examples, then Chapter 3 on weak derivatives and Sobolev spaces, and Chapter 4 on elliptic problems. Use Chapter 1 as a reference when an estimate requires a missing tool. The first substantial goal is to reconstruct one existence argument and identify exactly where its boundary condition, inequality and completeness assumption enter.

Preparation should be tested through arguments. Can you use Cauchy–Schwarz to bound an integral, distinguish pointwise from norm convergence, and explain why completeness matters for a Cauchy sequence? Can you integrate by parts in several variables and work with orthogonal projections? If so, review only unfamiliar measure theory and function spaces. If convergence theorems are still new, pause the PDE route long enough to learn their hypotheses and build counterexamples to false interchanges of limits and integrals.

Fill the analytical gaps

For that preparation, a real alternative is Sheldon Axler's Measure, Integration & Real Analysis. Chapters 2–3 build measure and integration; Chapters 6–8 connect Banach spaces, LpL^pLp spaces and Hilbert space geometry. Choose this route when the analytical foundations, rather than differential equations, are the main gap. If dominated convergence and orthogonal projection are already working tools, use those chapters selectively instead of restarting from Riemann integration.

A second alternative is Terence Tao's An Epsilon of Room, I, whose §§1.9 and 1.12–1.14 connect weak topologies, Fourier transforms, distributions and Sobolev spaces. This is useful for someone who knows basic measure theory but wants a broader picture of analytical estimates. Follow one question through those sections: how does controlling oscillation or derivatives improve the kind of convergence available? It supplies a different perspective from a course organized around boundary value problems.

What a weak solution does—and does not—say

Weak solutions are sometimes mistaken for inaccurate numerical answers. Weakness describes the meaning in which an equation holds, not a tolerance in its computation. An exact solution may lack classical derivatives. Likewise, existence in a chosen function space does not automatically prove uniqueness among every conceivable generalized solution. Whenever a theorem appears, record the space, the data assumptions and the estimate that supports its conclusion.

The next branch depends on the behavior you want to understand. Elliptic equations emphasize boundary problems and regularity; parabolic equations bring smoothing and long-time evolution; hyperbolic and dispersive equations emphasize propagation and oscillation. Variational problems and nonlinear equations introduce further compactness questions. Fourier analysis is a powerful companion for several of these directions, but there is no need to finish all of harmonic analysis before beginning a carefully chosen PDE problem.

Editorial choice and revision

Lebl gives equations to calculate and verify. Hunter is a short bridge to Sobolev language, while Dyatlov develops the distribution framework. The point is to choose the next step, not to start three courses at once.
